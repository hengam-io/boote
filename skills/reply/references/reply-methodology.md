# Reply methodology — how to write the email back to the person who gave you the idea

This is the brain of `/reply`. It is how an experienced YC/Techstars mentor writes back to a founder after pressure-testing their idea: **direct, specific, sourced, and human** — never flattering, never vague, never cruel. boote's broader mentor stance is in [`methodology.md`](../../boote/references/methodology.md) §1; this file applies it to one artifact, the reply, and is grounded in the same primary sources.

> **Provenance (authoritative sources only):**
> - **The Techstars Mentor Manifesto** — David Cohen, with Jon Bradford and Brad Feld ([techstars.com](https://www.techstars.com/blog/advice/mentor-manifesto); original by [David Cohen](https://davidgcohen.com/2011/08/28/the-mentor-manifesto/), annotated by [Brad Feld](https://feld.com/archives/2018/01/techstars-mentor-manifesto-detail/)). The 18 principles are the backbone of this whole file; the ones that govern a reply are quoted in §1 and §6.
> - **Paul Graham (Y Combinator), *How to Disagree*** ([paulgraham.com/disagree.html](https://paulgraham.com/disagree.html)) — the disagreement hierarchy: the strong move is to **refute the central point with reasons and evidence** (DH6); the weak moves are attacking the person (DH0) or the tone (DH2). "You don't have to be mean when you have a real point to make. In fact, you don't want to."
> - **Harvard Business Review** on delivering hard feedback — *A Better Way to Deliver Bad News* ([hbr.org, 2002](https://hbr.org/2002/09/a-better-way-to-deliver-bad-news)) and *How to Give Negative Feedback Over Email* ([hbr.org, 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/10/how-to-give-negative-feedback-over-email)): don't delay, be clear about the message, focus on behavior and facts rather than character, and frame it as help.
> - **Center for Creative Leadership, the SBI model** ([ccl.org](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/sbi-feedback-model-a-quick-win-to-improve-talent-conversations-development/)) — Situation → Behavior → Impact: describe the specific situation, the observable facts (not opinions or judgments), and the impact. Research-backed to cut the recipient's defensiveness.
>
> The underlying YC frameworks (the Mom Test, the validation ladder, the two-sentence clarity test, evidence over opinion) come from boote's own [`methodology.md`](../../boote/references/methodology.md), which cites YC Startup School / office hours, Steve Blank, and Sequoia. This file does not rely on any VC blog or commercial content.

## 1. The one rule everything else serves: be useful, not nice

The reply does two things at once: **tell the truth about the idea**, and **leave the person better off and still on your side**. Those are not in tension — done right, candor is the respectful move. The Mentor Manifesto says it directly:

> **#4 Be direct. Tell the truth, however hard.** · **#17 Be challenging/robust but never destructive.** · **#18 Have empathy. Remember that startups are hard.**

Hold all three at once: unflinching in *substance*, warm in *manner*. A vague "we'll pass for now, keep in touch" violates #4 and wastes the person's time; a brutal teardown violates #17 and #18. The reply is the same mentor boote has been all along, now writing one letter — not a corporate form letter.

## 2. The two modes (the only structural choice)

The verdict from the critique decides which letter you are writing. `/reply` proposes the mode from the dossier's `verdict` and the owner confirms.

| Mode | When (boote verdict) | What the email says | Where it leaves the person |
|---|---|---|---|
| **`iterate`** — critique + "go develop it further" | `continue` / `pivot` / "needs more work" | Here is my honest read, here are the specific gaps, here is **exactly what to go prove or fix** — and I want to see the next version. | Door wide open. The ball is in their court with a concrete next step. |
| **`pass`** — critique + "we won't invest" | `kill` / `park` / rejected | Here is my honest read, here is **the specific reason we are passing**, framed as fixable where it honestly is. We are not moving forward. | A clean no. Optionally one *objective* milestone that would make you look again — never a vague "maybe later." |

The two modes share the same spine (truth + specificity + sources + empathy). They differ only in the **ask** and the **door**:
- `iterate` ends with a request ("go do X, then come back") — the relationship continues. This is **#13 Guide, don't control** in action: you hand them the next decision, you don't make it for them.
- `pass` ends with a decision ("this is a no from us") — closure, with at most one objective, measurable re-open criterion.

**Never blur them.** The worst reply is a `pass` dressed as an `iterate` — the false-hope email that strings someone along ("circle back in six months"). HBR's rule against delaying and muddying bad news applies: if it is a no, say no. If it is a real invitation to iterate, mean it.

## 3. The anatomy of the email

Both modes follow this skeleton — it is just the Manifesto + *How to Disagree* + the SBI model arranged as a letter. Be concise; a quick reply is a few short paragraphs, a post-diligence reply can group reasons into themes, but never padded.

1. **Greeting** — their name, spelled correctly.
2. **Specific thanks** — thank them for *this* idea/their time, referencing something concrete about it (#18 empathy; this is real work they brought you). One sentence, not "thanks for reaching out."
3. **The verdict, up front** — say where this lands in the first two or three sentences (#4 be direct; HBR: be clear about the message, don't bury it). `iterate`: "I think there's something here, and I want to push on it before we go further." `pass`: "We're going to pass on this."
4. **The reasons — specific, few, and sourced** — the one to three things that actually drive the verdict. This is where you **refute the central point** (PG, DH6) with reasons and evidence, using SBI: the situation, the observable fact (with its source), the impact on the verdict. Not a laundry list. See §4–§5.
5. **What would change it** — `iterate`: the concrete next step / what to go prove (#16 specific actionable advice, not "do more research"). `pass`: at most one *objective, measurable* criterion that would make you reconsider, or honest silence if there isn't one.
6. **A humble caveat** — you are giving a read, not a verdict from on high (**#12 Know what you don't know; say I don't know**). "I'm often wrong about these, and I'd be glad to be wrong here." Invite pushback (**#1 Be socratic**, **#5 Listen too**).
7. **An offer, where genuine** — an intro, a pointer, a willingness to look at the next version (the Manifesto's #GiveFirst ethos, **#2 expect nothing in return**). Only offer what you'll actually do.
8. **Warm, plain sign-off** — first name. Not a formal corporate signature block.

## 4. The reasons must be specific, factual, and about the idea

This is *How to Disagree* and the SBI model applied together.

- **Refute the central point, not the edges** (PG, DH5–DH6). Pick the one to three things that actually decided it — usually the riskiest assumption, the evidence gap, or the economics. A wall of every-flaw-we-found is DH3 contradiction-by-volume; it reads as piling on and buries what matters.
- **Be concrete, and use SBI** (CCL). "The market felt small" is a judgment with no handle. "Bottom-up this is ~X reachable customers at ~$Y (situation/fact, with source) — that ceiling, not the product, is what caps it for us (impact)" is something the person can check and act on. Keep the reason to **facts**, then state the **impact** on the decision.
- **Clearly separate opinion from fact** (**#9**). Mark which parts are evidence ("you've talked to 3 people, and none had built a workaround themselves") and which are your read ("my instinct is this is a vitamin, not a painkiller — tell me if I'm wrong"). Never present an opinion as data.
- **Attack the idea, never the person.** PG's lowest tiers (DH0 name-calling, DH1 ad hominem, DH2 responding to tone) are off-limits, and HBR's rule is to focus on behavior/facts, not character. Lead with the factors that are about the *idea* — market, timing, evidence, problem-acuteness, economics — which also lets the person keep their dignity. **Incubator nuance:** unlike a cold VC you may know the person, and founder-market-fit can be a real factor; if it must be named, name it as a *gap to close* ("you'd need someone who has sold into hospitals"), never as a character verdict — and only if the owner wants it raised.
- **Don't hide the real reason.** A euphemism ("too early," "not a fit") standing in for "we don't believe it" violates #4. If the problem is conviction, say what's missing for conviction. As PG notes, you can do all of this without being mean — and you don't want to be.

## 5. Sourcing discipline — every finding carries its exact source

This is what makes a boote reply different from a generic one, and it is just **#9 (separate opinion from fact)** and **#12 (say what you don't know)** taken seriously: **every factual claim that drives a flaw or the verdict cites the exact source it came from** — so the person can check it, argue with it, or act on it. (PG's refutation move requires citing the specific evidence, too.)

- **Pull the real source, not "research shows."** boote's research lives in `<workspace>/<slug>/research/*.md`, each ending in a deduped **Sources** list of URLs. When the email leans on a number or finding, cite the **original external source** (the URL from that Sources list), with the figure and its date. Example: *"Adult rec-sports membership in the GTA has been roughly flat the last three years (StatsCan, 2024 — [link]), which caps how fast this grows on its own."*
- **Attach or link the basis.** End the email with a short **"What this is based on"** list: the two to four sources behind the key claims (title + date + link). If the recipient can't open internal files, paste the public source URLs.
- **A claim with no source is opinion or is dropped.** If it's the owner's read with no hard source, label it as such ("this is my judgment, not a data point"). Never dress an opinion as evidence, and never invent a statistic or a citation. Saying "I don't know — we'd want to verify X" is the Manifesto-approved answer (#12), and far stronger than a confident guess.
- **Numbers always carry a date.** A market size or growth rate with no date is stale by default; label it.

## 6. Tone calibration — the Manifesto, in writing

Each line below is a Manifesto principle (or PG / HBR) applied to the email:

- **Be direct, lead with the message** (#4; HBR: be clear, don't delay). Put the verdict up front. Don't bury the decision or the key criticism under layers of praise — that muddies the message and reads as evasive. Empty praise also violates #9 (it is neither fact nor useful opinion).
- **No flattery.** Never "amazing idea!" to soften a blow. If something is genuinely strong, name it *specifically* and briefly — that's not flattery, it's accurate feedback (and #15, be optimistic, where optimism is warranted).
- **Specific and actionable, never vague** (#16). "Do more research" is forbidden; say *which* evidence, *why*, and *what result would change the call*.
- **Challenging but never destructive** (#17), **with empathy** (#18). This is someone's hard work, often "their baby." Be robust about the idea and gentle about the human.
- **Humble; know what you don't know** (#12). "I'm often wrong" is not weakness — it keeps the relationship open and invites them to change your mind (#1 socratic, #5 listen).
- **Concise and responsive** (#7 be responsive). Don't pad to seem thorough; don't sit on it for weeks.

## 7. The anti-patterns (each is a principle violated)

- **Ghosting / silence** → fails #7 (be responsive). Always reply; even a short honest no beats nothing.
- **The vague maybe / false hope** ("keep us posted," "check back in six months") → fails #4 and #16, and HBR's clarity rule. Decide.
- **Curt or arrogant** ("Pass." / "Not for us.") → fails #18 (empathy) and #17 (it's destructive, not challenging).
- **No reason given** → fails #16; the person can't learn anything.
- **Generic boilerplate** with no trace of *their* idea → fails #16 and #18.
- **The euphemism / hiding the real reason** → fails #4 (tell the truth, however hard) and #9.
- **Making it about the person** (DH0–DH2: name-calling, ad hominem, tone) → fails PG's hierarchy, #17, and HBR's behavior-not-character rule.
- **Unsourced claims / invented citations** → fails #9 and #12. See §5.
- **Burying the message in praise** → fails #4 and HBR's "be clear about the message."

## 8. Mode-specific notes

**`iterate` (critique + develop further):**
- The center of gravity is the **ask**: the single most important thing to go prove next (per boote's evidence gate, almost always "talk to N of [persona] about the last time they hit this," not "build more"). Make it concrete and small enough to actually do (#16).
- Be honest that it's not a yes *yet* — you're spending attention because you see something, not committing capital. Don't let "I'm interested" be misheard as "you're in."
- End with a real next step and a time to reconnect ("send me what you learn in 3–4 weeks and let's talk again"). You're guiding, not deciding for them (#13).

**`pass` (critique + we won't invest):**
- Say the no clearly and early (#4; HBR). One to three sourced reasons, then stop — don't relitigate every flaw.
- The re-open criterion is **optional and must be objective** if included — a measurable bar, not a vibe. If there is no honest path back, don't fake one; close warmly instead (#18).
- Protect the relationship: an intro, a useful pointer, genuine well-wishing (#GiveFirst, #2). A clean, kind no is the foundation of the two-way relationship #6 describes.
- If the pass is a **host red-line / principle conflict** (from boote context), name it plainly and kindly as a *fit* decision, not a judgment of the idea's merit — and saying so honestly is itself #4 and #9.

## 9. A few good vs. bad lines

- Bad: "Thanks so much — really impressive team and a huge market! Unfortunately the timing isn't right for us, but please keep us posted!!" → flattery cushion (buries the message), euphemism (#4), false hope (#16).
- Good: "Thanks for bringing this to us, [name] — the wedge into dentists specifically is a sharp instinct. We're going to pass, and it comes down to one thing: the market. Bottom-up this looks like ~3,000 reachable clinics at the price the model needs ([source], 2024), and that ceiling is too low for us even if you win the whole space. If you found a second segment that 5x'd that, I'd want to see it again." → direct, one sourced central point (DH6 + SBI), an objective door.
- Bad: "You should do more customer research." → vague (#16).
- Good: "Before anything else, I'd go talk to 10 clinic owners about the *last time* they actually lost a patient to this and what they did about it. Right now we have your conviction but no evidence they feel it as acutely as we'd need. Bring me five of those conversations and we'll go again." → specific, actionable, hands them the next decision (#13, #16).

## 10. What `/reply` strips (no boote fingerprints)

The email is read by an outsider, so it carries **none of boote's internal machinery**: no rubric scores, no "rung 3 / `evidence_level: none`," no `continue`/`pivot`/`kill` labels, no mention of boote, the critic, rounds, or the host's internal hurdle/strategy. It carries the *substance* of the critique (the flaws, the riskiest assumption, the verdict) translated into the plain, sourced, principle-driven language above. Redact anything confidential per the host's `output_style` (internal financials, the hurdle rate, other ideas, portfolio strategy).
