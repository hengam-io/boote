## 1. The three rules

The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick) `[secondary]`: (1) talk about **their life and world**, not your idea; (2) ask about **specifics that happened in the past**, not opinions or hypothetical futures; (3) **talk less, listen more**. Why: everyone — especially your mom — will lie to be nice if asked to judge an idea; good questions ask for facts about the past, which even a biased person cannot answer falsely.
Sources: https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/ · https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/ (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 2. The question bank

**Bad (future / hypothetical / polite — never in a kit):** "Do you think it's a good idea?" · "Would you use/buy this?" · "How much would you pay for X?" · "Don't you think this feature is cool?"

**Good (past / specific / concrete):**
- "What's the hardest part about [doing X]?" — locates the sharpest pain (Migicovsky Q1).
- "Tell me about the **last time** you [hit X]." — anchors to a real episode (Migicovsky Q2).
- "Why was that hard?" — soften to "what made that hard?" / "what led to that?" (see §16.4); the answer is future marketing copy (Migicovsky Q3).
- "What, if anything, have you **done** to try to solve it?" — effort spent = severity; nothing tried = red flag (Migicovsky Q4).
- "What don't you love about what you've tried?" — maps the competitive gap (Migicovsky Q5).
- "Walk me through how you do [X] today." · "Do you use any tools/tricks/spreadsheets for it?" (Alvarez Q1–Q2)
- "Last time you did [X], what were you doing right before? Right after?" — workflow bracketing (Alvarez Q4).
- "If you could wave a magic wand and do anything you can't today, what would it be?" — the one licensed future-question; logged as opinion-grade, used to spot the underlying job (Alvarez Q3).
- "What did that cost you (time / money)?" · "Who else should I talk to about this?" · "Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?"

Sources: Migicovsky, "How to Talk to Users", YC Startup School 2019 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MT4Ig2uqjTc, details per https://medium.com/@davegoldblatt/startup-school-reference-guide-for-lecture-3-how-to-talk-to-users-with-eric-migicovsky-f4afd41b8e9d; Alvarez, *Lean Customer Development* `[secondary]` — https://howtoes.blog/2024/03/23/lean-customer-development-building-products-your-customers-will-buy-book-summary/; Mom Test lists per mtlynch.io above (all accessed 2026-08-14).

## 3. Data anti-patterns

`[secondary — mtlynch.io, accessed 2026-08-14]`
1. **Compliments** — costless, predict nothing; "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning" (Fitzpatrick). Deflect and return to their life.
2. **Fluff** — generic claims ("I usually…"), future promises ("I would…"), maybes ("I might…"). Anchor every fluffy statement to a concrete past instance.
3. **Unexamined feature requests** — never transcribe as truth; dig for the motivation underneath.
4. **Pitching / fishing for compliments** — the moment you explain the idea, they stop telling the truth about their problems. Migicovsky's three mistakes are the same list: pitching, hypotheticals, talking too much (reference guide above, accessed 2026-08-14).

## 4. Commitment and advancement

A meeting "went well" only if it ended in **commitment** (they gave up something of value) or **advancement** (a concrete next step). Currencies: **time** (a scheduled follow-up with an agenda, pilot hours), **reputation** (an intro to their boss/peers/partner, a testimonial), **money** (LOI, pre-order, deposit). Kind words with no commitment = a polite failure; log it as such. Blank's version: validation is an **exchange of value**, not praise.
Sources: mtlynch.io `[secondary]`; https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/customer-development-process `[secondary]` (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 5. Preparation

Before each interview `[secondary — mtlynch.io, accessed 2026-08-14]`:
- Agree the **three biggest unknowns** this conversation must inform (the current riskiest assumptions) — refreshed per segment as answers land.
- Pre-select the **commitment ask** you will make if the problem is confirmed (which currency).
- Do desk research first; never spend interview minutes on googleable facts.
- Pre-commit **what would count as validation vs invalidation** for each unknown, so results update the model instead of being rationalized (Blank — https://www.jumpstartinc.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Customer-Discover-Guide-01-02-18.pdf `[secondary]`, accessed 2026-08-14).

## 6. Sequencing

Steve Blank's customer discovery separates the **problem interview** from a later, distinct **solution interview**: first verify who the customer is and that the problem hypothesis is real ("get out of the building" — facts live outside the office); only then return with a solution presentation. Founders do the interviews themselves — never outsourced. A discovery kit therefore contains **no product description**; concept testing is a separate kit.
Sources: https://steveblank.com/category/customer-development/ · https://learn.marsdd.com/article/blanks-customer-discovery-method-part-2-the-customer-development-model-in-value-proposition/ `[secondary]` · https://www.entrepreneurship.org/learning-paths/the-lean-approach/getting-out-of-the-building-customer-development (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 7. Pass signals

Blank's **earlyvangelist ladder** `[secondary]`: the interviewee (1) has the problem → (2) knows they have it → (3) has actively searched for a solution → (4) has cobbled a workaround → (5) has or can get budget. Build with people at (4)–(5); a segment stuck at (1) fails the "now" test. An existing cobbled workaround is the strongest unmet-need signal; zero attempts to solve = the problem is probably not painful enough.
Sources: https://www.businessfloss.com/books/the-four-steps-to-the-epiphany · https://www.productbookshelf.com/2012/02/finding-your-customers-as-you-build-your-product/ (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 8. Timeline and four forces

JTBD switch interviews (Bob Moesta; Christensen's "hire a product to make progress" framing): pick a **real past purchase/switch** and reconstruct its timeline like a documentary, using concrete episodic anchors (when, where, who was with you, what device) to jog genuine memory instead of rationalization.

Phases: **first thought** ("when did you first think something had to change?") → **passive looking** ("what did you start noticing?") → **active looking** ("what did you actually compare / google / ask?") → **deciding** ("walk me through the day you decided; what almost stopped you?") → **first use** ("did it match what you expected?") → **ongoing** ("what does it do for you now?").

**Four forces:** a switch happens only when **push** (friction of the current way) + **pull** (imagined better life) exceed **anxiety** (performance/embarrassment/security worries) + **habit** (routines, switching cost). Teams over-invest in pull and ignore the two blocking forces. Pre-product, run switch interviews on the **solutions people already use** (incl. hiring a professional, a spreadsheet, doing nothing) — that reveals the forces a future product must beat.
Sources: https://jobstobedone.org/ · https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces/ · https://thehuman2ai.com/research/guides/jtbd-switch-interview/ (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 9. Interviewer craft

Deploy Empathy (Michele Hansen) `[secondary — https://howtoes.blog/2024/04/11/deploy-empathy-book-summary/, accessed 2026-08-14]` — "talk like a therapist":
- **Mirror** their last few words with a questioning tone; **summarize** neutrally ("It sounds like…") and let them correct you; **validate** ("that makes sense", "that sounds frustrating") without agreeing or disagreeing.
- **Soft, slow tone**; **3-beat pauses** after asking — people fill silence with the valuable part. Alvarez's harder version: after the first big open question, stay silent a full 60 seconds `[secondary — howtoes.blog LCD summary, accessed 2026-08-14]`.
- **Let them be the expert:** never correct them, even when factually wrong — the misconception is the data; adopt their vocabulary.
- Never talk about yourself, never defend, never sell.
- Phrase inventory: "Tell me more." · "Can you say more about that?" · "Walk me through that process." · "How long does that take?" · "What did you use before?" — plus Alströmer's neutral probes: "What do you mean by that?" · "Why is that important to you?" (https://www.horebinternational.com/talking-to-users-key-takeaways-from-gustaf-alstromer/, accessed 2026-08-14).
- Follow tangents for a minute or two — tangents flag what actually matters (Alvarez). Track **energy**: sighs, emphasis, pauses — probe where emotion spikes; emotion is prioritization (Alvarez; JTBD craft, thehuman2ai.com, accessed 2026-08-14).
- "Play dumb" deliberately — framing questions as your own confusion lowers defensiveness (thehuman2ai.com, accessed 2026-08-14).

## 10. Bias traps

- **Leading questions** embed the desired answer; participants mirror your words. Rewrites (NN/g): "I saw you struggling — what happened?" → "What was easy or difficult about that?"; "How much time would this save?" → "How might this affect your work, **if at all**?"; point neutrally and ask "what is that?" instead of naming/interpreting for them. Don't reveal the specific hypothesis up front — state a vague purpose ("how people go about [domain task]"), or answers get primed. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/leading-questions/ (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Facilitation mistakes** (NN/g): skipping rapport; failing to probe (keep a neutral-probe bank ready); facilitator multitasking on notes; a room full of observers (keep them out/silent); broad-to-narrow question order violated. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interview-facilitation-mistakes/ (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Social desirability bias:** people over-report virtuous behavior and under-report embarrassing behavior; mitigate with neutral open questions, normalizing framings ("other people tell me…"), and behavior questions instead of self-assessment. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social-desirability_bias · https://atlasti.com/guides/interview-analysis-guide/social-desirability-bias (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Recall bias:** memory drifts; ask about **recent, specific** episodes with contextual anchors (the JTBD technique). Source: https://www.looppanel.com/blog/8-biases-ux-researchers (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Hypothetical futures are aspiration, not prediction** — the one point Fitzpatrick, Migicovsky, Alvarez, and JTBD all share (sources in §1–§8).
- **Researcher-side confirmation bias:** pre-commit pass/fail per hypothesis (Blank, §5); once a theme repeats 3×, actively try to **break** it in later interviews ("other people tell me the opposite — what's your experience?") (Alvarez `[secondary]`, howtoes.blog LCD summary, accessed 2026-08-14).

## 11. Recruiting

`[secondary — howtoes.blog LCD summary; Alströmer sources §9; accessed 2026-08-14]`
- Define the segment by **behavior/circumstance** (recently did X, currently lives Y), never demographics alone; recruit people who lived the episode recently (less recall bias).
- Channels: warm intros with a **pre-written forwardable blurb**; LinkedIn by role; communities/forums where the segment already discusses the problem (contribute first); physical congregation points; Reddit/Slack/Discord and in-person events.
- Screener: 3–5 factual questions (did the behavior happen, when, their role in the decision) — without revealing the hypothesis. Record-don't-filter any attribute the owner wants tracked as a variable.
- Incentives: none for warm/professional contacts (attention + influence suffice; coffee or a small charity donation fine — Alvarez); a small gift card ($10–25) for cold consumer recruits (Hansen); see §16.2.
- Logistics: offer 3–4 concrete slots, promise 20–30 minutes, book one per hour (overrun + debrief room), send reminders, expect ~5–10% no-shows.

## 12. How many interviews

- **Alvarez:** plan **15–20 per segment**; first visible enthusiast by ~5; clear patterns by ~10 (if not, the segment is too broad); stop at saturation — when interviews stop surprising you `[secondary — howtoes.blog LCD summary, accessed 2026-08-14]`.
- **Blank / I-Corps:** the volume school — ~50-name contact list, 10–15 interviews/week while discovery is open; NSF I-Corps requires ~100 over 7 weeks (https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps/national-teams-applicants, accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Hansen:** ~5 interviews per **discrete question** ≈ 80% of the insight; cap 1–2 interviews/day (empathy fatigue) `[secondary — howtoes.blog DE summary, accessed 2026-08-14]`.
- **Kit synthesis:** first batch of 5 (calibration + first-enthusiast check) → review → 15–20 per segment before claiming a pattern; 3 independent mentions = theme candidate; saturation = stop; the count resets when the segment definition changes.

## 13. Remote vs in-person

Blank favors in-person early (read engagement); the Mom Test favors casual coffee-style chats over formal slots; Hansen finds remote fine and **audio-first often more candid than video**; NN/g: keep observers out of the live room regardless of medium (sources §3–§10, accessed 2026-08-14). Kit guidance: remote-audio/video by default for reach and recording; in-person for high-stakes personas and serendipitous conversations; identical scripts either way.

## 14. Note discipline

Separate three layers **at capture time**: **facts** (what they did / what happened), **verbatim quotes** (marked, with emotion flags), **interpretation** (your inference, clearly labeled) — mixing them is how bias enters the record. The facilitator is not the primary notetaker: record + transcribe, or bring a silent notetaker; handwritten beats typing for rapport when solo. Debrief within minutes: 5–7 bullets under **validates / invalidates / also interesting**, surprise level, commitment obtained, a self-audit for leading questions, and "what I wish I'd asked."
Sources: Alvarez + NN/g + Hansen + Mom Test pair rule (§1, §9, §10, accessed 2026-08-14).

## 15. Evidence grades

Grade every captured claim in the evidence file:

| Grade | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Committed behavior — they gave time / reputation / money or advanced | booked follow-up with agenda; intro made; LOI/pre-order |
| **B** | Verified past behavior — a specific, datable episode described in detail | the JTBD timeline of their last purchase |
| **C** | Current-state facts — tools, frequencies, costs stated about the present | "we pay $X/mo for Y"; "it takes me ~3h/week" |
| **D** | Opinions/feelings — generalized pains, preferences, feature wishes | pointers to dig, never proof |
| **E** | Hypotheticals/compliments — "I would…", "great idea" | logged only to be discounted |

**boote mapping:** only grades **A–C** support rung claims. `evidence_level: interviews:N` counts conversations that produced B/C-grade material; `commitments` requires accumulated A-grade; D/E never advance a rung.
Sources: Mom Test currencies (§4), Blank value-exchange (§4), JTBD timeline (§8), Alvarez grammar rule — active-voice past = signal, passive/future = noise (§14 source), accessed 2026-08-14.

## 16. Disagreements

1. **Counts:** Hansen ~5 vs Alvarez 15–20 vs Blank/I-Corps ~100 — different claim scopes (one discrete question vs a segment-level problem vs a whole business model). State which claim level a batch is validating.
2. **Paying participants:** Alvarez no (bias risk) vs Hansen yes for cold consumer recruits — split by relationship warmth.
3. **Formality:** Fitzpatrick's casual chats vs Blank's structured problem presentation vs NN/g semi-structured protocols — split by stage: casual while exploring, structured once segment + hypotheses are firm.
4. **"Why":** Migicovsky's Q3 is a bare "why was that hard?"; Hansen/Alvarez soften to "what led to…" (accusation risk). Same causal target — ship the softened phrasing.
5. **Alvarez's pattern threshold:** carry the graded milestones (enthusiast ~5 / patterns ~10 / plan 15–20), not the condensed "~10".
6. **Recording vs paper:** record when consent and formality allow; paper in hallway-style chats.
7. **Video:** audio-only can raise candor (Hansen) vs in-person for reading reactions (Blank) — persona- and stage-dependent.
